Fluency is the Final Frontier

📅September 30, 2026

We live in an incredible world. LLMs can solve millenium prize math problems. However, I cannot get them to do this because I am not fluent enough if fluid dynamics to really understand the fundamental tensions Navier Stokes is exploring. Even if I had a room full of fields medalist that would execute my ideas to a tee (even better than a LLM), it would be difficult for me to direct them to solve research math problems. Intelligence and reasoning are absolutely required advance the field of mathematics forward, but they are not enough. A deep understanding of the field is requisite too.

In this respect, I think software overlaps with math. It is incredibly that anyone can prompt or build a flashy demo; "build me twitter, make no mistakes" can take you surprisingly far with a sufficiently sophisticated model. However, when we want that same software to endure for years and under the weight of millions of users, we need experts that are fluent in the software who understand its weaknesses and bottlenecks. They will have fluency in the ideas and be able to describe problems and desired solutions more effectively than amateurs because they have the vocabulary amateurs lack.

I've been framing this in my mind as, humans must build fluency in ideas and fields in order to properly steer agentic systems. Even if agentic systems were fully autonomous in their execution of building software and were watching for reliability and performance signals and continuous shipping improvements to address problems as they encountered them, we still would greatly benefit from human experts. Those human experts would be free from the toil of maintenance and scaling the existing systems and could instead focus on working with other people to identify the next direction they want to take the software; they'd provide valuable input on the kinds of problems it would be able to readily and accurately solve and what kinds of problems it is ill-suited to.
