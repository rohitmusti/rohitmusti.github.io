Organic, Grassfed, Handwritten Code

📅June 5, 2026

There is zero doubt that LLMs are a large part of the future of writing code. The first several years of my career, LLMs weren't good enough to write code. I had a coworker (back in '22) who was bragging about how he could have LLMs write a few unit tests on his behalf and how much time that saved. But, they were very far from writing a substantial amount of high quality production grade code. In effect, vibe coding was a joke. In the years since, vibe coding has transormed from a joke into a credible replacement for parts of my job as a software engineer.

In part, I am excited. I can rip through six or seven different solutions to a problem without spending the associated effort. I can get a sense of how well a library can address a problem without having to thoroughly learn its interface before I sink my time deeply into the approach that seems the most durable. When I got married, I used LLMs to help me navigate writing all of the CSS of my wedding website so it could be beautiful. Heck, for this blog, it was bare HTML until I pointed a LLM at helping me improve its aesthetic (to make it easier to read). This has made me more effective at my craft and there is something addictive about seeing if I can one shot a problem while I grab coffee with a coworker.

Despite all of these tremendous gains, I don't know if I am happier or a better engineer when aided by LLMs. These are some open questions I am exploring as a I generate more code with LLMs instead of writing it by hand (as an organic, grassfed being).

What about the pace?

LLMs have dramatically changed expectations around how long a particular project might take. That is arguably a good thing, but I suspect it has compressed expectations too much. Before, there was time budgeted for (1) planning, (2) writing, (3) testing, (4) monitoring. In theory LLMs have reduced the writing cost. However, they have also reduced expectations around how much time and effort is spent planning, testing, and monitoring. I don't think this has been fully felt yet, but I am starting to observe the weight teams experience when trying to maintain and reason through code largely written by LLMs. It is awkwardly written, poorly tested, full of misleading comments, and often ignores convention. It is like an incredibly mid engineer ripped through a project with little oversight and now we are stuck cleaning up the mess.

I think the effect of this boils down to: the pace at which organizations are writing and deploying code is faster than their ability to make good decisions about the code and its design. This will have significant long term costs once the pain of those tradeoffs comes home to roost.

How will we evaluate design patterns?

As humans, when we write code on top of a system, we develop a feel for what parts of that system work well and what parts don't work well. My favorite example of this is when a new engineer who joined my team felt the pain of writing unit tests in our existing flow. He demonstrated to all of us the effectiveness of parametrized unit tests. That supercharged the team and led us all to increase the robustness of our tests (as it reduced the overall cost of writing them). When we outsource writing code to LLMs, I do not think we develop the same feel. Over time, this might weaken our judgment and the overall quality of our codebase.

This reminds me of a general theory that we need to do a lot of the foundational tasks in order to develop the pattern recognition to handle and reason through advanced concepts. I'd add on to that theory, if we stop doing the foundational tasks, how long before our pattern recognition deteriorates? For physical capabilities, if you don't maintain your fitness, you lose it. I suspect we are about to discover that the same is true for our mental capabilities. It is so little effort too! As humans, we need far fewer samples to develop strong pattern recognition (and eventually instinct) compared to LLMs.

I don't know for sure, but I think that the startups that are writting 100% of their code with LLMs today will be in significant pain in ~3 years when many people's expertise deteroiorates.

What about the love of the game? The joy of writing code?

I initially didn't want to be a software engineer, I wanted to study philosophy or politics or music or anything but sitting in front of a computer. However, in my first year of undergrad I discovered that writing code was an incredible way to get access to hard and interesting problems. Buried in the terminal are problems of massive complexity that I can describe in clear, structured language. I can boil that problem down to its literal bits as I search for a solution. There is a deep joy in that work and it is the reason I got into software engineering.

LLMs sometimes feel like a delightful partner who helps me see just past the edge of my knowledge, pointing me to a research paper or little known library that helps me bridge my understanding just enough to solve new problems. Other times they feel like an annoying righteous and uninformed prick that is constantly trying to pull me off the yellow brick path into a hellish poppy field full of complexity born from premature optimization and overuse of dependencies that don't actually exist.

How Well Do LLMs build on top of LLMs?

At my company, LLMs have largely been writing code on top of a codebase that a team of engineers poured lots of love and care into building. The codebase isn't perfect (it has bad neighborhoods) but it is largely coherent. That means that LLMs are almost always on solid footing. What remains to be seen is how well they will operate as the percent of code written by humans decreases and the percent of code written by LLMs increases: will the mistakes LLMs make compound into incoherence and downtime or is that prediction just cope as I (and other engineers) try to avoid a reality where our skills are rendered irrelevant by a new technology?

At the moment, I can't unsee the fundamental gaps. How is it possible that LLMs will replace me when they make fundamental mistakes on most tasks that require me to intervene? How will LLMs solve the problems around gathering organizational context (stated and unstated) and provide sound judgment on the right direction? Who is ultimately accountable for systems wholly built by LLMs? I don't see CEOs or product managers lining up to be on call. I suspect that the role of software engineers will involve writing less code and more time spent being accountable for and signing off on production systems.

What Does This Mean Going Forward?

I will continue to use LLMs to ease the burden of writing code. I hope it means I can spent more time on the parts of my role most important to me (developing expertise and being a good teammate) and less time on the unimportant parts of my role (writing the Nth unit test). I hope they mean that more things are possible and the pie grows larger. For now, there is still value in writing some code the slow, organic, grassfed way; not because it is the fastest way to generate text, but because it is how I (and other engineers) will develop and maintain our judgment on critical systems.
